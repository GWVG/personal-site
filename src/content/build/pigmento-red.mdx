Some buildings earn their form through compromise. This one earned it through constraint.

The site in Nijlen is a terraced plot with a kink — a slight but decisive bend that misaligns the street facade from the garden orientation. A noise-heavy railway line runs along the front. The plot is narrow. On paper, these are the conditions that produce forgettable houses: closed at the front, cramped at the sides, generic at the back. The brief instead became an argument for the opposite.

<InlinePhoto
  src="https://res.cloudinary.com/dkuy9qldr/image/upload/f_auto,q_auto/pigmento-red/back-side.jpg"
  alt="Back side of the Nijlen house, showing the monolithic volume with pigmented red zinc eaves"
/>

The response was a single monolithic volume in deep red — brick, roof tile, and zinc fascia all pulled from the same palette, down to a pigmented red zinc for the eaves. Not as a stylistic exercise, but because a fractured material language would have made the volume read as smaller and more compromised than it is. The monolith holds its ground between its neighbours without competing with them. The kink in the plot, rather than being absorbed or hidden, becomes the hinge around which the building rotates — the house turns its back on the street and faces its own garden with full conviction.

The incision into that volume — the notch that creates the entrance passage and separates the main body from the garden wing — is where the concept becomes spatial rather than just formal. Standing in that gap, between two high red walls narrowing toward the sky, the mass of the building becomes palpable. It reads less like a facade composition and more like a canyon carved from a single piece of material. The grey-green window frames and timber accents in the gate and surrounds are the only relief — warm enough to prevent severity, restrained enough to not dilute the idea.

Internally, the logic is straightforward: closed and quiet toward the street, open and light-filled toward the garden. The living spaces stack and step back as they rise, with the uppermost bedroom opening into the full height of the roof, lit by the volume's own geometry rather than by added skylights. The technical specification — underfloor heating, system D ventilation, geothermal heat pump — follows from the same principle of doing less with more coherence rather than more with more complexity.

I carried this project from first client meeting through design, planning permission, tender documentation, bill of quantities, contractor selection, and site supervision to handover. It is one of the few buildings I have designed where the finished thing matches what I imagined at the start — not because the execution was simple, but because the concept was clear enough to survive contact with reality.
