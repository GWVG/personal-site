I grew up around motorcycles without ever really being a rider myself. My father toured on BMWs, my older brother was the type who took apart small-displacement bikes in the basement. I rode pillion with my father a handful of times — enough to know the feeling, not enough to count.

When I got my licence in 2016, I already knew what I wanted. I'd found the Moto Guzzi V7 II online and drove to the Netherlands with my father for a test ride. Then found a dealer closer to home and bought it. Matte black, leather saddlebags that suited the bike perfectly, round headlight. Nothing like the BMW.

As a teenager I secretly dreamed of a Vespa — just for the look of it. That never happened. But the preference for Italian design stuck. Moto Guzzi and Vespa share the same parent company. Some preferences run deeper than you think.

## V7 II — 2016–2022

The V7 II is a simple bike. Air-cooled V-twin, narrow frame, spoked wheels. No riding modes, no traction control, no TFT display. You sit upright, you feel everything, and the engine sounds like it was designed in a different era — because it essentially was. Moto Guzzi has been building motorcycles in Mandello del Lario since 1921, and the V7 doesn't pretend otherwise.

I rode it for six years. The Flemish polders, the Ardennes, the Vosges with a friend on a Kawasaki who had a different definition of a comfortable cruising speed. A lot of solo afternoons with no particular destination. On good days, I even rode it to site visits. Showing up on a Guzzi at a construction site gets you exactly the looks you'd expect.

The one thing the V7 II was not built for was luggage. The leather saddlebags looked the part but had their limits — fine for a day trip, less fine for anything longer. After a while, that started to matter.

## V85 TT Guardia d'Onore — 2022–present

I sold the V7 II a few weeks after picking up the V85. It sat in the driveway with a for-sale sign long enough, but I didn't ride it again.

The V85 TT is a different kind of machine. Taller, more upright, proper luggage mounts, a full colour display. Built for distance. But I didn't choose it because it was practical — I chose the Guardia d'Onore because I couldn't stop looking at it. Black with a single white stripe running the length of the tank. The name cast into the side panel: *Guardia D'Onore 1946*. There's a small Italian flag on the windscreen. Some people find that kind of thing excessive. I don't. It went to Scotland the first full season I had it.

The Guardia d'Onore is a limited edition. 1946 units, each with its production number engraved into the triple clamp. The number refers to 1946 — the founding year of the Italian Republic, and the year the Reggimento Corazzieri, the mounted honour guard of the Italian president, first chose Moto Guzzi as their official motorcycle. They've ridden Guzzis ever since, all of them built in the same factory in northern Italy. Mine is number 1013.

A few weeks ago, someone approached me while I was parking the bike. She said she was looking for a motorcycle and found this one beautiful. I told her what it was called. She probably bought a Honda.
