## Part One — The hardest part

We met in the car park of a Carrefour Market in Brecht, my father coming from Lichtaart, me from Lier. A compromise location chosen with the logistical efficiency of two people who would rather spend their kilometres on Scotland. It is perhaps the least romantic departure point in the history of motorcycle travel, but it was practical, and practicality is what gets you out the door.

The ride to IJmuiden was unremarkable by design. You don't sightsee on day one; you cover ground. By half past one we were at De IJmond Brasserie, eating what felt ceremonially like a last meal before casting off — though it was really just a decent lunch before a ferry crossing.

The cabin on the King Seaways was small, windowless, and equipped with a stacked bunk that left no one in doubt about the vessel's priorities. We spent most of the crossing on the observation deck at the bow, watching the North Sea do its thing, which that evening happened to involve a genuinely beautiful sunset. You take what you get.

## Part Two — Nobody shouted land ahoy

The English coast appeared the next morning. Nobody on deck shouted anything nautically appropriate (this was the DFDS Newcastle service, not a clipper ship) but you could tell from the way people suddenly found reasons to move around that land had appeared. After a night at sea, apparently, the sight of a coastline is enough to make otherwise reasonable adults pace the deck like they've somewhere to be.

From Newcastle we rode north through Northumberland: rolling, impossibly green, the kind of landscape where Teletubbies would live contentedly in their hills. At the Scottish border, someone has placed a large rock with the word "Scotland" on it, presumably for those who missed the road signs. We stopped for the obligatory photograph, taken by a fellow motorcyclist—a Brit who was visibly envious when we told him we were riding all the way north.

Jedburgh came next, and its abbey graveyard was atmospheric enough that I found myself half-expecting Peter Pettigrew to appear from behind a headstone, cradling the remnants of you-know-who. It was a beautiful sunny day. This did not make the graveyard less Gothic.

At the Forth Bridge near Queensferry we ran into the Dutch motorcycling couple we'd spoken with on the ferry that same morning. The world, as it reliably turns out, is small. After a brief stop at the Kelpies, two thirty-metre steel horse heads rising from the Falkirk flatlands with the quiet confidence of public art that has thoroughly earned its place. We checked into our hotel in Grangemouth.

## Part Three — Merely a preview

Day three brought 298 kilometres and a lesson in Scottish meteorological irony: as the landscape became progressively more interesting, the weather became progressively less cooperative. The moors around Cairngorms National Park were rendered in a grey that was, admittedly, very on-brand for the location. It was, as we would come to understand, merely a preview.

Day four delivered Loch Ness: we pulled on our rain gear, I wiped the lens of my camera, and the famous monster remained undetectable beneath what was, in fairness, not excellent visibility. A brief stop at Fort Augustus for a sandwich from the tourist shop. An obligatory stop, executed without sentiment. That evening, at our guesthouse in Tain, a rack of local flyers caught our eye. Clynelish Distillery. A "Flavour Journey." We booked it for the following morning.

## Part Four — The edge of things

By half past ten the next morning we were at Clynelish Distillery in Brora. They gave us a tour, a tasting, and a presentation case of bottles to take home on the motorcycle. The Flavour Journey: recommended.

Onward, north. John o' Groats we passed with a wave (it is, in the end, a car park and a signpost) and pressed on to Duncansby Head, which actually delivers on the geography. Sheep, dramatic rock stacks dropping into the sea, and a species of seabird called a Guillemot that looks, with its upright posture and dark-and-white colouring, exactly like a penguin that has gotten considerably lost. Also: wind.

The Strathy Inn that night was basic in the manner of a place that has correctly identified that its customers (motorcyclists, hikers, people who have run out of road) are not primarily there for the amenities. This far north, "desolate" is not a complaint. It's a description.

Somewhere south of Durness, the road follows the River Dionard inland. For twenty kilometres or so, there is nothing alongside it but the river. No houses. No car parks. Just the occasional passing place sign on the side of a poorly-maintained strip of tarmac threading through a landscape that has declined, decisively, to accommodate human presence.

I pulled over. My father did the same, a few seconds behind me as always. I took a photograph, though I'm not sure what I was trying to capture. The absence of something doesn't photograph easily. It was the first time on the trip I got goosebumps.

## Part Five — The west coast, and why it has to be grey

Day six continued west along the north coast before turning south: the causeway at Kyle of Tongue, Smoo Cave (genuinely worth it, tourists notwithstanding), the long descent toward Ullapool. At Kylesku Bridge, a small food truck sat at an otherwise deserted viewpoint. We stopped. The owner's card terminal had difficulty finding a network signal, which in that location was entirely understandable. We waited. It connected. Snacks were obtained. The transaction felt like a minor victory over geography.

Available accommodation being what it was, we instead rode forty kilometres inland to Inchbae Lodge in Garve: a hotel where the décor had stabilised somewhere in the early 1970s and appeared content to remain there.

Here's the thing: it was correct. That kind of weather belongs on that kind of coastline. Grey on the northwest coast of Scotland isn't the absence of atmosphere — it is the atmosphere.

## The Bealach na Bà

The Bealach na Bà — the Pass of the Cattle — climbs from sea level to 626 metres in a matter of kilometres. The ascent takes you into cloud. At the top, visibility is limited.

<InlinePhoto
  src={imgBealachViewpoint}
  alt="The descent from the Bealach na Bà"
  caption="Bealach na Bà viewpoint. The view is out there somewhere."
/>

Then you begin the descent, and as the road drops below the cloud layer, the valley appears — gradually, then all at once. A steep rock face on the opposite side, growing in scale as you descend, until it fills your entire field of vision.

I rarely feel overwhelmed by the scale of the natural world. The Bealach na Bà was the second time on this trip I got goosebumps. The cliff face took up the whole sky.

After that, Lochcarron was a warm welcome at the end of a cold, wet day: a hotel with a view of the water and a decent enough dinner. We slept well.

## Part Six — The long ride south

Day eight took us south through Fort William and into Glencoe, which has been dramatic since before anyone was there to appreciate it. We took a detour along Glen Etive, famous as the approach road in the Bond film Skyfall. It was raining. We went anyway. There are places that earn the effort of arriving wet, and Glen Etive is one of them.

Glasgow received us without ceremony: a city that never performs for visitors, and is better for it. We caught the Riverside Museum in the afternoon.

Glasgow to Newcastle via Carlisle was, by the standards of the preceding nine days, unremarkable. The North Sea terminal at North Shields was the same process in reverse: bikes in the hold, Scotland retreating behind you in the dark. IJmuiden to Lier the following morning was a corridor, not a journey. You turn off the engine in the driveway and there's a silence that sits differently from normal silence.

±2,650 kilometres. Eleven days. One small case of whisky, intact.

## Epilogue — What Scotland actually is

People ask whether it's worth it: the rain, the detours, the basic accommodation at the edge of the known world, the food truck whose terminal takes three minutes to find a signal. The answer is obviously yes, but that's not quite the right framing. Scotland on a motorcycle isn't a holiday you survive — it's a place that asks something of you, and the asking is the point.

The moments I remember most clearly aren't the famous ones. Not Loch Ness. Not John o'Groats. The moments are a stretch of empty road following a river where there was genuinely nothing for twenty kilometres. A cliff face appearing out of cloud on a descent that hadn't finished impressing you yet. A food truck at a deserted viewpoint, card terminal blinking, waiting for a signal.

One thing Scotland taught me: rain is relative. Back home, I used to check the forecast before taking the bike. Since Scotland, I've stopped doing that. Whatever it's doing out there, it's not Scotland. It's fine.
